Prompt: Create a self-contained, aesthetically pleasing, breathtaking animation of optogenetics: how a light-sensing protein from green algae became a light switch for brain cells. Use JavaScript on a single canvas, all in one HTML file. Be detailed and scientifically accurate. It should evoke awe.

Structure it as one continuous journey through scales, in chapters, with a short caption at each stop explaining the science in plain language:

1. The alga. A single Chlamydomonas reinhardtii cell (about 10 µm) swimming toward sunlight, beating its two flagella. Show the cup-shaped chloroplast and the orange eyespot that senses light.

2. The eyespot. Zoom into the eyespot membrane and show channelrhodopsin proteins embedded in it.

3. The molecule. Zoom into one channelrhodopsin-2: seven transmembrane helices with a retinal chromophore inside. When a blue photon (~470 nm) arrives, the retinal flips from all-trans to 13-cis, the channel opens within about a millisecond, and positive ions (Na+, H+, Ca2+) pour through. Then it closes again.

4. The transfer. A modified virus (AAV) carries the channelrhodopsin gene into a chosen neuron. The neuron reads the gene, builds the protein, and places it in its outer membrane.

5. The switch. A thin optical fiber delivers pulses of blue light. Each pulse depolarizes the membrane and fires an action potential that travels down the axon, precisely timed to the light. Also show the opposite switch: a yellow-light-driven chloride pump (halorhodopsin) that silences the neuron.

6. The circuit. Zoom out to a network of neurons. Light drives one type of inhibitory neuron and produces gamma rhythms at around 40 Hz, visualized as synchronized waves (Cardin et al., 2009).

7. The brain. Zoom out to a whole mouse brain to suggest the scale: humans have about 86 billion neurons, and optogenetics lets researchers choose which cells to switch, and when, to the millisecond. Briefly show two results from the article: well-timed light lengthening hippocampal sharp-wave ripples to improve memory (2019), and a closed-loop system that detects a seizure and stops it with light (2013).

8. The eye. End with the human retina: in 2021, a man with retinitis pigmentosa partly regained sight after surviving retinal cells were made light-sensitive, using goggles that project amber light. Close on the thought that it all began with curiosity about how a tiny green cell finds the light.

Style: a dark, cinematic palette where the light itself is the hero, glowing blue and amber against deep greens and blacks. Use elegant serif typography with minimal on-screen text. Add smooth easing between chapters, a scale indicator, play/pause and restart buttons, and scroll or drag to move through it manually. It should be responsive down to mobile and respect reduced-motion settings.

Accuracy matters more than spectacle. Keep the colours, sizes, timings, and molecular details true to the real biology. Do not suggest that optogenetics can read or write thoughts, and make clear it is still mainly a research tool.
